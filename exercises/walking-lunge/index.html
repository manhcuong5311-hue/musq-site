Walking Lunge

The walking lunge trains the quads and glutes one leg at a time while you travel forward. Every step adds a balance and coordination challenge, which makes it a strong all-round leg exercise.

Primary muscles
Quadriceps, Gluteus Maximus
Equipment
Dumbbell
Level
Beginner
Sets × reps
3 × 10–12 steps per leg
Rest
60–90 s

How to do the walking lunge

  1. Stand tall holding a dumbbell in each hand at your sides.
  2. Take a long step forward so your front knee will stack over your ankle.
  3. Lower your back knee straight down until it lightly grazes the floor, torso upright.
  4. Drive through your front heel and mid-foot to bring the back leg forward into the next step.
  5. Keep alternating legs for the set number of steps.

Form cues and common mistakes

The same coaching points MUSQ shows on the lifter in the app.

Step Length

Step length sets up everything else in a walking lunge.

Why it mattersA stride long enough to let both knees reach roughly 90 degrees at the bottom keeps the front shin close to vertical and the back knee under the hip, not way out behind it.

Common mistakeTaking a short, narrow step that forces the front knee far past the toes to reach depth.

Do this insteadStep out far enough that the front knee stacks over the ankle and the back knee drops straight down toward the floor.

Front Knee Tracking

The front knee is doing most of the steering in this lift.

Why it mattersTracking the front knee over the foot keeps it from twisting while the quads and glutes take the load at the bottom of each step.

Common mistakeLetting the front knee cave inward toward the other leg as fatigue sets in over the set.

Do this insteadTrack the front knee straight ahead over the foot, stacking directly above the ankle at the bottom of each step.

Torso Position

The torso has to stay stacked over the hips through every step of a walking lunge.

Why it mattersAn upright torso keeps the centre of gravity balanced between the two feet, which is what makes a walking lunge stable to travel on.

Common mistakeLeaning forward from the hips to help momentum carry the body into the next step.

Do this insteadKeep the chest lifted and the torso vertical, letting the legs — not a forward lean — do the work of moving forward.

Forward Drive

Each step ends the same way it should: driving forward off the front leg.

Why it mattersPushing hard through the front heel to bring the back leg through keeps continuous tension on the working leg instead of relying on a bounce off the back knee.

Common mistakePushing off the back toe to launch forward, which lets momentum finish the rep instead of the front leg's muscles.

Do this insteadDrive through the front heel and midfoot to bring the trailing leg forward into the next step.

Controlled Balance

A walking lunge is a moving exercise, and control has to travel with it.

Why it mattersLightly tapping the back knee near the floor rather than dropping into it keeps tension on the legs continuously between steps.

Common mistakeLetting the back knee bounce off the floor to rebound into the next step, trading control for momentum.

Do this insteadLower the back knee under control until it lightly grazes the floor, then drive straight into the next step.

Walking Lunge FAQ

Walking lunges or stationary lunges?

Walking lunges add balance and momentum control because you travel with every rep; stationary lunges are easier to load and fit in a small space.

How long should each step be?

Long enough that both knees bend to about 90° at the bottom. Short steps load the front knee more; very long steps shift work to the glutes and hip flexors.

Do walking lunges build the glutes?

Yes. The glutes of the front leg work hard to stand you up out of each step, especially with longer strides.
