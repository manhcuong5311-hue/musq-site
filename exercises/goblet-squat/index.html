Goblet Squat

The goblet squat is the best way to learn to squat. Holding one dumbbell against your chest keeps your torso upright and your core braced, so you can sit deep with good form while training the quads and glutes.

How to do the goblet squat

  1. Hold a dumbbell vertically against your chest, both hands cupping the top end.
  2. Stand with your feet about shoulder width, toes turned out slightly.
  3. Brace your core and sit down between your knees, keeping your chest tall.
  4. Let your elbows travel down inside your knees at the bottom.
  5. Drive through your whole foot to stand back up.

Form cues and common mistakes

The same coaching points MUSQ shows on the lifter in the app.

Goblet Hold

Holding the weight at the chest is what makes the goblet squat such a good depth and posture teacher.

Why it mattersA single load held centred in front of the chest keeps the whole body's centre of gravity balanced over the mid-foot without any barbell setup.

Common mistakeLetting the weight drift down toward the belt or out away from the chest, pulling the torso forward and off balance.

Do this insteadCup the weight against the sternum with both hands, elbows pointing down, and keep it pinned there the whole set.

Elbow Checkpoint

The elbows have a job at the bottom of a goblet squat that no other squat variation asks for.

Why it mattersLetting the elbows slide just inside the knees at full depth gently pushes the knees out into good tracking and confirms the squat is deep enough.

Common mistakeSquatting with the knees drawn together too narrow for the elbows to fit between them at the bottom.

Do this insteadSit down between the knees until the elbows lightly touch the inside of the knees at the bottom of the rep.

Torso Position

The goblet hold naturally teaches an upright torso.

Why it mattersBecause the load sits in front of the chest instead of on the back, staying tall is what keeps the weight balanced — lean forward and it pulls straight out of the hands.

Common mistakeRounding forward at the bottom, which is usually a sign the ankles or hips are short on mobility rather than a strength problem.

Do this insteadKeep the chest lifted through the whole squat, breathing into the belly to help hold the position.

Knee Tracking

Good knee tracking is easy to feel in a goblet squat because the elbows give it instant feedback.

Why it mattersLetting the knees travel out in line with the toes — helped by the elbows pressing them apart at the bottom — keeps the load centred in the knee joint.

Common mistakeKnees caving in as the squat gets deep, closing the gap the elbows are meant to fill.

Do this insteadPush the knees out over the toes as you descend, using the elbows at the bottom as a checkpoint.

Full Depth

Full depth is where the goblet squat earns its reputation as a mobility and pattern-building tool.

Why it mattersSitting all the way down until the elbows meet the knees opens the hips through their full range and builds the squat pattern other variations build on.

Common mistakeStopping at a shallow quarter squat, which skips the mobility and glute work the position is meant to build.

Do this insteadSink until the elbows brush the inside of the knees, then stand back up through the whole foot.

Goblet Squat FAQ

Is the goblet squat good for beginners?

Yes. The weight in front acts as a counterbalance, which makes it easier to sit deep with an upright back. It's a great way to learn squat technique before moving to a barbell.

How heavy should a goblet squat be?

Use a weight you can hold comfortably at your chest for 10–15 reps. When holding the dumbbell becomes the limit, move on to a barbell or split squat.

Goblet squat or back squat?

The goblet squat is easier to learn and very quad-focused; the back squat lets you load much heavier for strength.
